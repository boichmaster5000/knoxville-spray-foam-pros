How the Calculator Works

Spray foam is priced by volume, and the industry unit for volume is the board foot: one square foot of foam, one inch thick. Every estimate, ours included, starts from three numbers. How many square feet need foam, how many inches thick it has to be, and what a board foot of that foam costs installed.

The thickness comes from the R-value you want divided by the foam's R-value per inch. Open cell foam rates about R-3.6 to R-3.8 per inch across the Department of Energy guide and one manufacturer's data sheet. Closed cell rates about R-6.0 to R-7.4 per inch, depending on the blowing agent and product. The calculator uses both ends of each range, which is why it shows a thickness range instead of one number.

The price comes from This Old House's April 2026 cost guide, which lists one inch of open cell at $0.44 to $1.50 per square foot and one inch of closed cell at $1.25 to $2.90 per square foot, installed. One inch over one square foot is one board foot, so those are effectively per board foot prices. The low end of the cost range pairs the thinnest likely foam with the lowest price, and the high end pairs the thickest with the highest.

A worked example

Say you want R-13 of closed cell foam on 1,000 square feet of crawl space walls and band joist. At R-6.0 to R-7.4 per inch, that takes roughly 1.8 to 2.2 inches. That is about 1,760 to 2,170 board feet. At $1.25 to $2.90 per board foot, the ballpark lands near $2,200 to $6,300. Prep work, a vapor liner on the floor, or an ignition barrier coating would add to it.

Now try the same 1,000 square feet as an attic roof deck in open cell at R-38. The foam would need to be about 10 to 10.6 inches deep, which is more than many roof deck installations use. That is the calculator doing its job: it shows you when a target is expensive or impractical, so the conversation at your estimate starts in the right place.

What R-Value Should You Enter?

The calculator starts each application at a sensible default you can change. The defaults follow Tennessee's statewide energy code, which uses the 2009 insulation table for Climate Zone 4: R-38 for ceilings, R-13 for framed walls, and R-10 continuous or R-13 in the cavity for crawl space and basement walls. Some cities have adopted newer codes with higher numbers, so treat the code minimum as a floor rather than a goal.

Roof decks are the tricky one. The residential code's unvented attic section requires at least R-15 of air-impermeable insulation against the roof sheathing in Zones 4A and 4B when it is paired with fluffy insulation below, and it treats that layer as condensation control, not as the whole energy target. ENERGY STAR, meanwhile, recommends R-49 to R-60 for Zone 4A attics. How a foam roof deck satisfies the energy code for a particular house is something to confirm with your local codes office, and we talk it through at the estimate.

For metal buildings, the goal is often condensation control more than a code number. Installers commonly put at least 1.5 to 2 inches of closed cell on the steel for that purpose, which works out to roughly R-9 to R-15 depending on the product.

Why Your Real Quote Will Differ

The calculator prices foam. A real job prices a building. These are the most common reasons a written quote lands above or below the ballpark:

  • Removal and prep. Old attic insulation, wet batts in a crawl space, or rodent debris have to come out before foam goes in.
  • Access. Low roof pitches, tight crawl spaces, tall metal buildings that need lifts, and long hose runs all add labor.
  • Coatings and barriers. Foam in occupied spaces and many attics and crawl spaces needs a thermal barrier or an ignition barrier coating, which is a separate line item.
  • Pass limits. Closed cell goes on in passes of about 2 inches with cooling time between them, so thick closed cell takes more crew time per board foot.
  • Job size. Setup costs are the same for a small job and a big one, so small jobs cost more per square foot and often carry a minimum.
  • Air sealing and detail work. Sealing top plates, penetrations and attic hatches before or alongside the foam is labor the board foot math does not capture.
