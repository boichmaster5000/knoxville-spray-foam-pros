Closed Cell vs Open Cell Spray Foam for Tennessee Homes

Closed cell belongs where foam meets the ground, bare steel or a surface cold enough to collect condensation: crawl space and basement walls, rim joists, metal buildings and pole barns. Open cell is usually the better value where there is depth to fill and the assembly can dry, such as interior walls, deep framing and many attic roof decks. The roof deck is the one spot where either foam can be right, and the guide below shows how we decide it room by room.

Attic Roof Decks: The Zone 4A Question

This is the call that draws the most debate, and both foams are legal here. Knox and the surrounding counties sit in Climate Zone 4A. Under IRC R806.5, an unvented attic has to be inside the building's thermal envelope, cannot have a Class I vapor retarder on the ceiling side, and, when foam is the only insulation, the foam has to be in direct contact with the underside of the roof sheathing. In the 2015 IRC text, a Class II vapor retarder on the foam is required only in Zones 5 through 8, so it is not required in Zone 4A.

The case for closed cell. DOE's spray foam guide lists closed cell as preferred on sloped roof decks and in unvented attics in every climate. It slows vapor at 1 inch and stiffens the deck. It also suits a flash and batt roof, where Table R806.5 requires R-15 of air impermeable insulation against the deck in Zone 4A before fiber goes underneath; roughly 2 to 2.5 inches of closed cell covers that layer.

The case for open cell. DOE lists open cell as acceptable on sloped roof decks and in unvented attics, and it costs less per R. For humid climates, the guide pairs it with a vapor permeable interior finish so the assembly can dry. The stories of roof sheathing rotting under open cell come mostly from colder zones, where winter vapor drive pushes indoor moisture into the deck. In Zone 4A, building science writers who have studied those cases put the risk lower, though not at zero.

The thickness catch. DOE notes that the allowable open cell depth on a roof deck depends on the product's evaluation report, anywhere from 5.5 to 10 inches. At about R-3.6 per inch, reaching R-38 takes more than 10 inches, so many open cell roof decks are approved through a performance or alternative compliance path instead of the prescriptive table. Closed cell reaches R-38 in roughly 5 to 6.5 inches, sprayed in several passes. The City of Knoxville enforces the 2024 IRC, so we confirm the current R806.5 wording and the accepted compliance path with the plans reviewer before pricing a sealed attic.

Rules that apply to either foam. One manufacturer's unvented attic guide requires any gas furnace or water heater in the sealed attic to be sealed combustion or power vented, and an attic used for storage needs the foam itself covered by a thermal barrier. Ignition protection is product specific: one open cell data sheet calls for a listed intumescent coating in attics, while one closed cell product passed testing that lets it go into attics without a separate ignition barrier. Walls, Foundations and Rooms

Exterior walls with open framing

In an open 2x6 wall, open cell fills the cavity at roughly R-19 to R-22 for much less money than closed cell, well past Tennessee's R-13 wall minimum. A 2x4 wall changes the math. Open cell lands near R-12 to R-14 there, while one closed cell data sheet lists R-23 at 3.5 inches. DOE's guide prefers closed cell for frame walls in all climates, and industry testing reported in trade press found 2 lb closed cell raised racking strength by 70 to 200 percent, depending on the sheathing. Whether that is worth the premium depends on the wall and the budget.

Interior walls and sound

Between bedrooms, bathrooms and offices, open cell is the better of the two foams for sound, but the margin over fiberglass is thin. The Gypsum Association reports that in wood framed walls every insulation type adds about 2 to 4 STC points over an empty cavity, with no clear winner. A second layer of drywall or resilient channel changes a wall far more. See soundproofing insulation for those options.

Crawl space and basement walls

Closed cell. DOE's guide prefers it for foundations and below grade spaces and marks open cell as not acceptable there, and one open cell data sheet rules out below grade use and contact with bulk water. Building Science Corporation's crawl space sheet does list open cell as a wall option, so you will hear both opinions. In damp East Tennessee crawls we side with DOE. Tennessee's minimum for these walls is R-10 continuous, which closed cell reaches in a little under 2 inches on paper.

Two things come before the foam. Standing water, bad grading and gutter problems get fixed first, because foam sprayed over wet masonry locks the moisture in. And we keep the top of the foundation and the sill visible for termite inspection, following DOE's 3 inch inspection gap guidance; pest control trade press reports that foam over the sill can complicate termite inspections. More on the full system is on our crawl space insulation and crawl space vapor barrier pages.

Rim joists

Closed cell in most houses. Building Science Corporation says both 2 lb closed cell and 0.5 lb open cell work at the rim, with open cell needing extra vapor control only in Zone 6 and colder. We still lean toward closed cell because 1 inch of it already acts as a Class II vapor retarder against a surface that stays cold all winter. DOE's crawl space guide recommends about 1.5 inches, and the residential code lets rim joist foam stay uncovered up to 3.25 inches thick when it meets the density and fire test limits. Details are on the rim joist insulation page.

Garage ceilings under bonus rooms

Closed cell sprayed to the underside of the subfloor, or dense-packed cellulose, are the two common answers. Whatever goes in has to stay in contact with the floor above, and the rim around the room has to be sealed. The garage ceiling drywall is also a fire separation, so it goes back up properly afterward. Our garage and bonus room page goes further.

Metal Buildings, Pole Barns and Shops

Closed cell. On a clear night, steel panels drop several degrees below the air inside the building, and humid air that reaches them turns to water on the underside. Closed cell sprayed straight onto the panel keeps its inside face above the dew point and blocks the air in a single layer. A metal construction trade article warns against open cell on metal because it can shift framing and panels, and DOE notes that open cell absorbs and holds water while closed cell does not.

On thickness, 1 inch of closed cell is already an air barrier and a Class II vapor retarder on one manufacturer's sheet. Installers commonly spray 1.5 to 2 inches under roof steel, which is practice rather than code. In a shop or warehouse where people work, the foam still needs a thermal barrier or an approved coating.

Two honest caveats. Foam bonds permanently to the panels, so replacing a dented or rusted panel later means cutting the foam out with it. And the stiffness boost that closed cell gives wood framing is much smaller in a thin panel I-beam metal building than in a post frame barn, according to industry data reported in trade press. See metal building spray foam and pole barn insulation for how those jobs run.

Roof Leaks and Inspection

Spray foam on a roof deck changes how leaks get found. DOE's spray foam guide warns that with closed cell on the deck, a leak does not show through the foam, so it is harder to spot and trace. Because foam thickness is tied to the design of the roof assembly, re-roofing or repairing a damaged deck can mean cutting foam out. Open cell is not leak proof either. It soaks up and holds water, so a slow drip can hide in it too, although contractors often say open cell shows a stain sooner.

Before we spray any roof deck, we look from the attic side for old water stains, soft sheathing and daylight, and we ask how old the roof covering is. If the shingles are near the end of their life, replacing them first is usually the smarter order. After the job, look into the attic once a year and after big storms, and call a roofer at the first sign of a drip.

Common Mistakes We See

  • Spraying closed cell too thick in one pass. One data sheet caps each pass at 2 inches, or 3 in a single pass, and requires the core to cool below 100 F before the next one, because heavy lifts trap heat.
  • Open cell on crawl, basement or other below grade walls.
  • Open cell sprayed directly on metal roof or wall panels.
  • A flash and batt roof with less closed cell than the R-15 that Zone 4A requires against the deck.
  • Sealing an attic around a gas furnace or water heater that draws its combustion air from the attic.
  • Burying flexible ductwork in foam, which both data sheets prohibit.
  • Covering the sill plate so termite inspectors cannot see it.
  • Leaving foam exposed in a finished room or storage attic without a thermal barrier.
