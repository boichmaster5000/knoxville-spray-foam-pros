Spray Foam vs Fiberglass Insulation in Knoxville TN: The Real Comparison

Updated May 2026. Written by people who install both. No vendor bias.

Spray Foam or Fiberglass? It Depends on the Spot

Should you use spray foam or fiberglass in your Knoxville home? Use spray foam where air leaks and condensation are the real problem, and fiberglass where there is room for depth and the air sealing can be handled as its own step. In practice that puts foam on crawl space walls, rim joists, metal buildings and any roof deck you plan to seal, and blown or batt fiberglass on an open attic floor and in framed walls you can reach. Most houses end up with some of each.

Below we walk through the house one location at a time, then set the two materials side by side with a source for every number. Costs quoted here are national guide ranges we checked in October 2026, not our prices.

Location by Location

Attic floor in a vented attic

Fiberglass usually wins here on cost, as long as the ductwork runs below the ceiling and the attic will stay vented. Blown fiberglass fills the odd gaps around joists and wiring more completely than batts can. One manufacturer's coverage chart calls for at least 13.5 inches of its blown fiberglass to reach R-38, the statewide ceiling minimum, and 17 inches to reach R-49. ENERGY STAR suggests R-49 to R-60 for Zone 4A attics, so going deeper than the minimum is worth pricing.

The weak point is air. DOE's homeowner insulation guide says to find and seal attic leaks before adding insulation, and its air sealing guide explains why: insulation slows heat flow, but it does not block the air that rises through top plates, light fixtures and plumbing chases. We seal those first, then blow.

Roof deck, when the attic gets sealed

If your furnace or air handler and its ducts sit up in the attic, sealing the roof deck pulls all of that equipment inside the conditioned part of the house. Fiberglass cannot do that job by itself. The unvented attic section of the residential code, R806.5, calls for air impermeable insulation tight against the underside of the sheathing. When fiber insulation is part of the roof assembly, Zone 4A needs at least R-15 of air impermeable insulation against the deck, or rigid insulation above it. So a sealed roof deck means spray foam, either full depth or as a closed cell layer with fiberglass under it.

Open wall framing

For an addition, a new build or a gut remodel with the studs exposed, fiberglass batts are the budget choice and they meet code. Tennessee's wall minimum is R-13, and R-13 and R-15 batts are made to fill a 3.5 inch 2x4 bay. Batts only deliver their label value when they are cut to fit and left unsqueezed. One manufacturer's own fact sheet says an R-19 batt pushed into a 5.5 inch 2x6 cavity performs at R-17 to R-18, and the fiberglass industry's trade group warns that compression always costs R-value. Air sealing at plates, electrical boxes and wire holes is a separate job.

Open cell foam in that same 2x4 bay comes out around R-12 to R-14, depending on the product, and it seals the cavity as it goes. Closed cell packs far more R into each inch, which matters in shallow framing, at a noticeably higher price.

Crawl space walls

Foam board or spray foam, not fiberglass. Once a crawl space is sealed, its walls become the edge of the house, and Tennessee's minimum for them is R-10 continuous or R-13 in a framed cavity. Building Science Corporation's crawl space sheet lists rigid foam board and spray foam for those walls and leaves fiberglass off. DOE's spray foam guide marks open cell as unacceptable for foundations and below grade spaces, so our crawl wall work uses closed cell.

Floors over a vented crawl space

Fiberglass batts stapled or wired up under the subfloor are a common setup in vented crawls, and they are legal: the floor minimum is R-19, the label on a standard 6.5 inch batt. Our summers work against them, though. Building Science Corporation explains that crawl space ground in the South usually stays cooler than the outdoor dew point, so the humid air that drifts in through the vents leaves water on the plastic and on the insulation under the floor. DOE's crawl space guide says vented crawls in mixed humid climates like ours tend to add moisture rather than remove it, and it sets 17 percent as the upper limit for wood moisture content.

If the crawl stays vented, the batts need to sit tight against the subfloor with enough support that they cannot sag. Where the budget allows, sealing the crawl and insulating its walls is usually the better fix. In DOE's Building America research, sealed and conditioned crawls cut heating and cooling energy by 15 to 18 percent against vented ones.

Rim joists

Foam is the better material at the rim. Building Science Corporation names the rim joist as one of the worst air leaks in a typical house, and because its inside face runs cold all winter, fiberglass pushed into the bays can let moist indoor air condense on the wood behind it. DOE's rim joist guidance recommends rigid foam cut to fit and sealed at the edges, or two part spray foam. Its crawl space guide calls for about 1.5 inches of closed cell at the band joist, and foam up to 3.25 inches thick can stay uncovered there without a thermal barrier as long as the space is not living space.

Metal buildings and pole barns

Closed cell foam. Steel panels cool several degrees below the inside air on clear nights, and warm, damp air that touches them condenses and drips. A metal construction trade article points out that air leaks carry far more moisture into a roof or wall than vapor diffusion does, so faced fiberglass only works if the facing and every seam stay sealed for the life of the building. One manufacturer's data sheet shows its closed cell foam acting as an air barrier and a Class II vapor retarder at just 1 inch, bonded right to the metal. Installers commonly spray 1.5 to 2 inches under roof steel. That is field practice, not a code number.

Using Both: Flash and Batt and Other Hybrids

Flash and batt means a thin coat of closed cell foam sprayed against the sheathing to stop air, with fiberglass filling the rest of the cavity. DOE's spray foam guide recommends at least 1.5 inches of closed cell in cold climate hybrid walls and at least 1 inch in hot, humid ones. East Tennessee falls between those two, in the mixed humid zone, so we plan on the thicker figure.

On a roof deck, the closed cell layer has a firm minimum. Table R806.5 calls for R-15 of air impermeable insulation against the deck in Zone 4A, which works out to roughly 2 to 2.5 inches of closed cell, before fiberglass goes in below to bring the roof up to the energy code R-value. That table is a condensation control floor. It does not replace the energy code requirement.

Flash and batt walls tested at STC 34 in the NAIMA series whether the flash coat was open or closed cell, the same score as an R-15 batt alone. The hybrid is about air and moisture, not quieter rooms.

The other kind of hybrid is spending foam money only where it does something fiberglass cannot. A typical plan for an older ranch house looks like this: closed cell on the rim joist and crawl walls, careful air sealing on the attic floor, then deep blown fiberglass on top. ENERGY STAR's national estimate for sealing and insulating attics, crawl space floors and rim joists is a 15 percent drop in heating and cooling costs, about 11 percent of the total energy bill. That is an average across many homes, not a forecast for yours.

Where Each One Goes Wrong in the Field

Both materials fail more often because of how they were installed than because of what they are made of. These are the problems we look for when we inspect older work, and the ones we work hardest to avoid in ours.

Fiberglass

  • Compressed or gapped batts. Squeezing a thick batt into a thin cavity lowers its R-value, and gaps around wires and boxes let heat slip past the batt entirely.
  • No air sealing underneath. Fiber slows heat, but air still moves through it and carries heat and moisture along.
  • Shallow blown attics. Blown fiberglass bags list both a minimum depth and a minimum bag count; one chart calls for 19 bags per 1,000 square feet to reach R-38. If either number comes up short, so does the R-value.
  • Insulation against hot fixtures. One manufacturer's instructions keep loose fill 3 inches away from recessed lights that are not made for insulation contact, and clear of flue clearances.
  • Floor batts left unsupported. Under a crawl space floor they sag away from the subfloor and stop doing much.

Spray foam

  • Off ratio or badly mixed foam. Contributors on building science forums describe foam that stays sticky or crumbles, usually traced to the wrong mix ratio, cold material or lifts sprayed too thick, and some of it keeps smelling until it is cut out.
  • Lifts that are too thick. One closed cell data sheet limits each pass to 2 inches, or up to 3 in a single pass, and requires the core to cool below 100 F before the next pass to prevent heat buildup.
  • Spraying onto cold or damp surfaces. The same sheet wants the surface at least 5 degrees F above the dew point. In a humid crawl space or on bare steel, that takes measuring, not guessing.
  • Foam over flexible duct. Both foam data sheets we reference say not to bury flexible ductwork.
  • Exposed foam in living space. The residential code requires a thermal barrier, such as 1/2 inch drywall, between foam and the room.
  • Hiding the sill from termite inspectors. DOE's rim joist guidance keeps a 3 inch inspection gap where termites are a concern.
  • Coming home too soon. EPA notes that many manufacturers call for about 24 hours before occupants return after two part foam.

What Tennessee's Code Minimums Mean for This Choice

Tennessee's statewide residential energy code is the 2018 IECC with state amendments that took effect July 16, 2020. Those amendments put the older 2009 insulation table back in place, so in Climate Zone 4 the prescriptive minimums are R-38 for ceilings, R-13 for wood frame walls, R-19 for floors, and R-10 continuous or R-13 in a cavity for crawl space and basement walls.

For comparison, the base 2021 model code asks for R-60 in a Zone 4 ceiling. The statewide numbers are a floor to build up from.

Local editions differ. The City of Knoxville enforces the 2024 IRC alongside the 2018 IECC, while Maryville moved to the 2024 IRC and the 2024 IECC on January 1, 2026, which makes its insulation minimums stricter than the state table. Check the code for your address before you settle on R-values.

Here is how that plays out. Fiberglass meets the wall and attic floor minimums cheaply: an R-13 batt in a 2x4 wall, or a deep layer of blown fiberglass on the ceiling. Roof deck foam is harder. Reaching R-38 with open cell alone at about R-3.6 per inch takes roughly 10.5 inches, and many roof decks are sprayed thinner and approved through a different compliance path. That is a conversation to have with the local inspector before the job starts, and we raise it at the estimate.

Meeting code on paper also assumes a good install. A compressed or gapped R-13 batt is not delivering R-13, and the gap between label and reality is often wider than the gap between materials. If you are upgrading anyway, ENERGY STAR's R-49 to R-60 for Zone 4A attics makes a better target than the R-38 minimum.
