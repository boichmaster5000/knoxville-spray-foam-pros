Injection Foam vs Blown-In Insulation for Existing Walls in East Tennessee

If your older East Tennessee house has empty exterior walls, either injection foam or dense-pack blown-in insulation can fill them without tearing out drywall, and the wall itself usually decides which one fits. Injection foam costs more, seals air as it fills the cavity and comes with a published per-inch R-value. Dense-pack cellulose usually costs less and is a method weatherization crews know well, but it slows air rather than stopping it. Some walls cannot take either one, and we cover those below.

Side by Side

This question comes up often around here because so much of the housing is older. Census figures show 67.3 percent of Oak Ridge's housing units and about 58 percent of Morristown's were built before 1980. The injection foam numbers below come from one manufacturer's tech sheet and blog; we leave product names off on purpose. The blown-in numbers come from attic charts, government guides and national cost guides, and we flag every spot where our sources have no wall-specific figure.

R-Value: Read the Fine Print

Per inch, injection foam has the edge on paper. At R-4.59 per inch, a full 3.5 inch 2x4 cavity holds about R-16 of foam, and the maker's whole-wall calculation, which counts the studs that interrupt the insulation, comes to R-15.7 for a 2x4 wall. Both figures clear Tennessee's R-13 minimum for new wood frame walls. Commenters on one building science forum argue that real framing drags the whole-wall number down further than that, so treat the maker's figure as a best case.

Blown-in is harder to pin down, and we would rather say so than guess. Every per-inch number in our sources comes from attic work: about R-3.65 per settled inch for one loose-fill cellulose, about R-3.7 for cellulose in DOE's homeowner guide, and about R-2.5 for loose fiberglass. Dense-pack is a different material condition. At about 3.5 pounds per cubic foot, it is more than twice as dense as the attic layer on that cellulose chart, which works out to roughly 1.5 pounds per cubic foot by our arithmetic. Density changes R per inch, so the attic figure does not carry over, and none of our sources publish a wall-specific dense-pack value. Blown fiberglass made for walls has the same gap. If an installer quotes a wall number, ask for the data sheet behind it.

Where the Holes Go

Neither method can be done from the attic. Both go in through holes drilled into every stud cavity, from outside or from inside the room, and the exterior finish decides the approach. The steps below describe how injection foam typically goes in by siding type; dense-pack uses the same access points.

  • Vinyl or aluminum siding: the crew removes one course of siding, drills a 2.5 inch hole through the sheathing into each cavity, probes up and down for blocking, fills from the top down and then from the bottom up, plugs the holes and reinstalls the siding.
  • Wood lap siding: sound boards come off and go back on. Brittle boards are drilled straight through and the holes filled, which leaves some paint touch-up for the homeowner.
  • Brick veneer: three 5/8 inch holes per cavity go through the mortar joints near the bottom, middle and top. The holes are pointed back up with mortar, and saving the drilling dust helps the patches blend in.
  • Stucco: three 5/8 inch holes per cavity, closed with mortar afterward. The texture affects how visible the patches end up.
  • Fiber cement boards: each board is lifted, nails are pulled or cut, and the boards are refastened and caulked afterward. That takes noticeably more labor than vinyl.
  • Concrete block: holes go into the aligned cores partway up the wall and are mortared closed.
  • From inside: the room is covered in plastic, holes are drilled through drywall or plaster along a straight line, then plugged and given a rough coat of joint compound, ready for the homeowner's final finish and paint.

Hole size is one place where injection foam has a real advantage on masonry: it can go through a hole as small as 5/8 inch, which fits inside a mortar joint. Stone veneer usually means working from inside instead. Our sources give no standard hole size for dense-pack, so ask any installer what diameter they drill and where on the wall the holes will land.

Settling, Shrinkage and Missed Cavities

Loose cellulose settles in an attic. One manufacturer's chart implies about 7 percent between installed and settled depth, and a building science editor tells installers to plan on 13 percent. In a wall, settling would leave a gap at the top of each cavity, which is exactly what dense-packing is meant to prevent.

Packed to about 3.5 pounds per cubic foot, cellulose stops settling, according to building science sources. The risk is a crew that does not pack each cavity hard enough, leaving room for the material to slump over time. Density is something you can ask about and check.

Injection foam does not expand once it is in place. It fills the cavity under pump pressure and sets in 10 to 30 seconds. Some early shrinkage is considered normal, and how much is where opinions split: one inspector on a building science forum described a wall where injected foam had pulled back noticeably, while makers say current products hold their shape. For comparison, one masonry-core injection product lists shrinkage under 1 percent.

Missed spots are the other risk for both methods. Blocking between studs, wiring and pipes can stop the fill partway up a cavity. That is why the drill pattern matters, why injection crews probe for blocking and time each fill, and why an infrared camera on a cool day is worth using afterward. DOE describes the same kind of thermal imaging in its home energy assessment guidance, where leaks show up as dark streaks.

Moisture and Drying Time

Injection foam goes in wet. The product on our data sheet starts as a dry resin powder, gets mixed with water and foamed with compressed air, sets in seconds, and needs at least 48 hours for final drying. Anything on the wall that blocks moisture slows that drying, including interior plastic sheeting, many layers of old paint or vinyl wallpaper. Keep indoor humidity down while it dries, and mention any of those finishes at the estimate.

Once dry, the foam stays vapor open at 6.63 perms per inch, so the wall can still dry in either direction. That suits Climate Zone 4A, where the residential code does not require an interior vapor retarder and does not permit a Class I barrier such as plastic sheeting.

Cellulose has its own moisture trade-off: it soaks up water if a window, flashing or siding leak reaches it, so those leaks get fixed first. With either method, we look for signs of existing moisture before filling a wall. Insulation hides a leak; it never fixes one.

Fire and Sound

In the ASTM E84 tunnel test, the injection foam on our data sheet posted a flame spread of 25 and smoke developed of 40, which puts it in Class 1. A masonry-core injection product lists flame spread 0 and smoke 5. Cellulose is treated with fire retardants, and one maker says its treatment is entirely borate based, but we did not find a cellulose data sheet with E84 numbers we could quote. Ask any installer for the data sheet of the exact product going into your walls.

For sound, expect a modest change either way. The injection foam tech sheet lists STC 41 and a 45 to 50 dB loss in a 3.5 inch cavity between 500 and 4,000 Hz, and the masonry product lists STC 53 in block. Those results depend on the whole wall that was tested. A gypsum industry association found that in wood stud walls, every insulation type adds roughly 2 to 4 STC points over an empty cavity, with no clear winner. Windows, doors and outlets usually let in more noise than an insulated wall.

Walls Where Neither One Works

  • Cavities already packed with old cellulose, especially the glued-in kind. There is nowhere for new material to go.
  • Walls with no sheathing behind the siding, where injected foam has nothing to hold it in the cavity.
  • Solid brick walls, brick on brick, with no cavity to fill.
  • Thin wood paneling, which can pop loose under injection pressure.
  • Weak lath and plaster, where careless filling can blow out a section. We test these walls before committing.
  • Walls with an active leak or rot. The water gets fixed first.
  • Open framing such as rim joists, crawl spaces and overhangs. These are not enclosed cavities, so they get spray foam instead.

Walls with some old insulation are a judgment call. Compressed old batts can usually be foamed around, while loose fill can block both foam and dense-pack. A small test hole tells us which case we are dealing with. Open areas belong on our rim joist insulation and crawl space insulation pages.

Wiring deserves a word too. The foam maker says its product stays soft enough to fish a wire through later, while building science forum posters point out that injected foam sticks to studs, sheathing and the back of the drywall, which makes later demolition messier. If an electrical update is on your list, do it before the walls are filled.

What It Costs

Injection foam has no national price list; the maker leaves pricing to local dealers. As one data point, a Michigan dealer's October 2025 cost post put all four exterior walls of a house at $5,400 to $10,900, and another page from the same dealer estimated about $4,000 to $8,000 for a 1,500 square foot house. Those are another region's prices, not ours.

For dense-pack cellulose, one national cost guide last updated in 2022 lists $2.00 to $2.30 per square foot. A newer national guide from August 2025 puts blown-in cellulose at $1.75 to $3.80 per square foot installed, a range that includes attic work. Building science forums generally describe dense-pack as the cheaper of the two methods for the same walls.

Siding type moves the price as well, because lifting vinyl takes far less labor than handling every fiber cement board or cedar shake. For local ranges across all our services, see the Knoxville insulation cost guide. Your free estimate gives a written price for the method that fits your walls.

Injection Foam vs Blown-In FAQ

Done right, neither should change much, but both depend on the crew. Dense-pack cellulose packed to about 3.5 pounds per cubic foot does not settle, while under-packed cavities can slump and leave a gap at the top. Injection foam does not expand, some early shrinkage is considered normal, and field reports of larger shrinkage do exist, so product choice and mixing matter.

Only one course of it, and it goes back on. The crew removes a row of vinyl or aluminum siding, drills through the sheathing into each stud cavity, fills the wall, plugs the holes and puts the siding back. Holes for injection foam through sheathing run about 2.5 inches. Brick and stucco houses skip this step, since the holes go through mortar instead.

An infrared camera scan on a cool day is the simplest check, because an empty or short-filled cavity shows up as a different temperature band between studs. DOE describes the same thermal imaging in home energy assessments. Before the job, ask how the crew finds blocking, how they confirm each fill, and, for dense-pack, what density or bag count they target per wall.

It can be done, but find out why that wall feels cold first. A thermal scan often shows that other exterior walls are empty too, and DOE lists outlets, switch plates, window frames and baseboards among the most common air leak points, which can make one wall feel worse than it is. Filling all the exterior walls at once usually makes more sense than coming back later.
